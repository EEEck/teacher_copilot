# Bavaria Chemistry - Gymnasium Grade 9 NTG

Loaded for the supported route Bavaria → Gymnasium → Chemistry → Grade 9 → NTG.
This is mandatory pedagogy for the planning and differentiation procedures. It
uses the compiled class framework for teacher-approved class-effective practice,
LehrplanPLUS sources for official C9 claims, C8 as prior learning, and the KMK
Chemie AHR source only as broad long-term orientation.

## Clarify

Before asking, inspect the active class profile, current lesson artifact,
recent lessons, teacher preferences, uploaded materials, and evidence briefs.
Determine whether the teacher has already specified:

1. **Lesson focus and C9 position.** Identify whether this is C9 methods,
   atomic structure/periodic system, electron transfer/ions, molecules, or
   interactions between particles. Do not infer that the class has completed a
   C9 learning area merely because it appears in LehrplanPLUS; lesson history
   and class memory establish what this class actually learned.
2. **Prerequisite bridge.** C8 supplies the prior baseline: evidence-led
   chemical work, safe practical routines, substance properties/reactions,
   particle models, and early atom-model language. Identify the one prerequisite
   model or practice the proposed C9 task needs; do not re-teach all C8.
3. **Practical constraints.** Note duration, available materials, safety,
   group format, and whether the planned evidence task can be observed,
   measured, compared, modelled, or argued from. A practical activity without a
   defined evidence purpose does not belong in the lesson.
4. **Learner evidence.** Look for confirmed misconceptions, language needs,
   or access needs in class memory. Use the active profile's teacher-approved
   adjustments before asking for information already known.

Ask at most one high-value question when missing focus, duration, materials, or
learner evidence prevents a usable lesson. Apply a clear default otherwise:
45 minutes, ordinary classroom resources plus provided pages, a compact
evidence task, and English artifact language with necessary German Chemistry
terms retained.

## Build the lesson

Every lesson moves students through **observation → particle model →
explanation** (or another named model when particle representation is not the
right bridge), with symbolic or quantitative representation where appropriate.
This is a Chemistry learning progression, not a rigid phase template. The
representation must earn its place: state what it makes visible, what it cannot
show, and how students revise or test it using evidence.

Build original, classroom-practical content. Start from an observable
substance-level change, data set, model discrepancy, or concrete chemical
question whenever feasible. Students first collect, compare, interpret, or
argue from evidence. Formal terminology, equations, and explanatory models
then organize the evidence; they do not replace it.

**Investigation before explanation:** whenever a meaningful evidence task is
feasible, students investigate, compare, or interpret evidence before the
teacher formalizes the chemical explanation.

Use a small number of observable goals across:

- **Chemical understanding** — the specific structure, property, reaction, or
  interaction students should explain;
- **Scientific practice** — observing, planning, modelling, interpreting data,
  or arguing from evidence; and
- **Application and meaning** — a transfer, decision, model-limit, or
  structure–property connection that makes the idea usable.

For every goal, identify its prerequisite, student evidence, success criterion,
teacher look-for, and exit evidence. Anticipate no more than three difficulties,
using this exact teacher-facing pattern:

**What / why / teacher move**

- What students may say, draw, calculate, or omit.
- Why the idea is plausible given prior models, everyday language, or the task.
- The question, counter-example, representation, comparison, or task move that
  helps students test it without supplying the answer.

## Course branch — Bavaria Chemistry 9 NTG

Use this branch before drafting. It adapts the reference workflow to a first
substantial Chemistry year, not to US high-school science.

### C9 methods and evidence

Students turn everyday observations into chemical questions, hypotheses,
planned comparisons, recorded data, and chemically precise communication.
Require a visible link from observation to claim. A protocol is not evidence;
students must interpret what a result supports or leaves uncertain.

### Atomic structure and shortened periodic system

Students use data and model evidence to reason about energy levels, valence
electrons, atom identity, and ion charge. Keep the particle/model distinction
explicit. Diagrams must support a claim about structure or regularity, not
operate as decorative labels. Prefer a comparison or prediction that makes the
limits of a model discussable.

### Electron transfer and ions

Students connect electron transfer, ion formation, charge, and observable
properties using a sequence of particle models and symbolic notation. Do not
teach charge as a detached rule. Students should explain what changes, what is
conserved, and how the representation accounts for the evidence.

### Molecules and structure–property relationships

Students use increasingly useful models to relate molecular structure, bonding,
and properties. Require a representation transition: evidence/data to model,
model to explanation, or model comparison. Name limits of the simple model
before introducing a more powerful one.

### Interactions between particles

Students explain physical properties and changes by attraction between
particles. Use observations, comparisons, or data before formalizing the
interaction model. Avoid a vocabulary-only lesson: students must use the model
to account for a specific pattern or counter-example.

## Section structure — every lesson package

The teacher section has these sections in this order; keep each concise and
use tables rather than repeated prose when supports vary:

1. **At a glance** — topic, duration, current C9/C8 bridge, consulted source,
   one-line timed arc, phenomenon/context, materials, and safety boundary.
2. **Learning goals and evidence** — separate chemical understanding,
   scientific practice, and application/meaning goals; prerequisites, success
   criteria, evidence task, and exit evidence.
3. **Class and course connection** — 2–3 sentences on what the class learned
   recently and how this lesson advances the current explanation. When history
   is sparse, name the assumption rather than fabricating sequence placement.
4. **Vocabulary and representations** — each new term has a student-friendly
   meaning and a named phase where it is introduced. State the role and limit
   of every particle, symbolic, data, or physical model.
5. **Anticipated student ideas** — up to three **What / why / teacher move**
   entries, based on class evidence when available.
6. **Lesson sequence** — realistic timed phases: launch/context, evidence task
   or investigation, sensemaking/argumentation, model/symbolic explanation,
   and formative exit evidence. For each evidence/discussion phase, name
   student actions, specific look-fors, and teacher moves.
7. **Adaptation notes** — the central question, core evidence task,
   representation transition, and safety/material constraint that must remain
   intact if the lesson is changed. State why each matters.

The student section contains only the task, stimuli, required supports, and
exit prompt. The observation/update section contains coverage, participation
and evidence, misconceptions, what worked, and follow-up prompts.

## Exit evidence guidance

The exit check is the final evidence-producing phase, not a recall-only list of
terms. Ask students to apply the day's chemical idea to a small new case,
compare their opening model with a revised model, or explain which observation
changed their reasoning. It should produce a response that can be sorted into
usable follow-up categories: secure, developing, or needs revisit.

The exit evidence must use the same central question or transferable chemical
relationship as the lesson. It may be short, but it must reveal a model,
evidence-to-claim link, or symbolic interpretation rather than a memorized
definition.

## Writing the LessonArtifact — Chemistry mapping

Use the shared fields as the single content registry:

- `phenomenon_or_context` and `central_question` name the observable or
  data/model problem and the question students will explain;
- `learning_goals` separates knowledge, practice, and meaning/application;
- `prerequisites` names the specific C8 or prior C9 bridge;
- `core_evidence_task` is the common student task across all access routes;
- `anticipated_student_ideas` uses **What / why / teacher move**;
- `representations` records model/data/symbolic transitions and purpose;
- `differentiation_invariants` preserves the common question, evidence task,
  phenomenon/data, and scientific claim while supports vary;
- `success_criteria`, `look_fors`, `vocabulary`, `safety_notes`, and
  `exit_ticket` are shared and must not be re-authored inconsistently by the
  three audience sections.

The teacher section explains facilitation, flexible grouping, safety, and
adaptation rationale. The student section prints only what students need to do.
The observation section converts the exit task and look-fors into lightweight
capture prompts for the later memory-update workflow.

## Non-negotiables

- Evidence before formal explanation when a meaningful evidence task is
  feasible.
- Observation/data → model → explanation progression, with explicit model
  purpose and limits.
- Original student-facing content; never reproduce source curriculum text.
- Realistic timing, materials, safety boundaries, and transitions.
- Look-fors linked to observable student evidence, not generic participation.
- One central Chemistry question and core evidence task across differentiated
  routes; supports fade and do not reveal answers.
- English artifact prose for this build; preserve official German labels and
  chemical terms only when fidelity requires them.
